Build-Along · Remotion + Claude Code

Kodak invented the future, then filed it away.

The same six-prompt machine that built the Netflix-vs-Blockbuster reel, pointed at a different story — one engineer, one prototype, and the most expensive filing cabinet in history.

K Build-along kit  ·  ~12 min read

This is the second reel off the same rig. Everything structural — the Remotion project, the 12fps posterize step, the film treatment, the six-scene shape — is identical to the Netflix build. All that changes is the story and the skin. That's the whole point of building it as an engine: the second reel costs a fraction of the first.

The story: in 1975 a 24-year-old Kodak engineer named Steven Sasson wired a CCD sensor to a cassette deck and built the world's first digital camera. It weighed eight pounds, shot 0.01 megapixels, and took 23 seconds to record one black-and-white image. He showed it to management. Their response, as he tells it, was "that's cute — but don't tell anyone about it." Kodak filed the patent, locked the idea in a drawer, and kept selling film. The patent expired in 2007. Kodak filed for Chapter 11 in 2012.

The rule, again

The voiceover is the source code. Six lines, six scenes. Lock the script before you open an editor — the words decide what's on screen, when it cuts, and how long it holds. Every prompt below is downstream of those six sentences.

The Storyboard

Before a single line of code, you write the script and hand it to Claude Code as a storyboard brief. The move is the same as last time: you don't describe visuals — you give it the voiceover and let it derive them. "Don't tell anyone" forces a dismissive executive. "No film, no prints, no waiting" forces three headlines. "Locked it in a drawer" forces a literal drawer. The VO is the storyboard.

Paste this in as-is — the whole 30-second script is baked in, so there's nothing to fill in:

✦Claude CodePrompt · Storyboard
▸I'm making a 30-second vertical documentary reel that tells the Kodak vs the digital camera story. Here's my voiceover — six lines, one per scene: 1. In 1975, a young Kodak engineer built the world's first digital camera. 2. He showed his bosses. They told him it was cute — but don't tell anyone about it. 3. So everyone else built the opposite. No film. No prints. No waiting. 4. By 2012, Kodak had filed for bankruptcy and switched off its film plants for good. 5. That same year, Instagram sold for a billion dollars — thirteen employees, not one roll of film. 6. Kodak invented the future, then locked it in a drawer. Turn this into a storyboard. For every line, tell me the scene it becomes — what we're looking at, the words that go on screen (pulled straight from the line), the emotional beat, one signature animation, and the exact assets I'll need to make it. Give it to me scene by scene so I can build one at a time.

Every scene also carries the same three threads throughout: the stop-motion "posterize" judder on all motion, the vintage film-treatment wash, and — new for this reel — an optional sprocket-hole edge. All three are set up once, in the next two prompts.

You don't need an original story. Kodak's collapse is one of the most-retold cautionary tales in business — that's fine, and it's the same reason Netflix-vs-Blockbuster works. The magic isn't finding a story nobody knows; it's taking one that already lands and telling it sharper than the last person did.

00The Setup & the Engine

The storyboard tells you what to build. Before you build any of it you set the project up once, and build a small reusable engine all six scenes pull from. Two parts:

  • A motion toolkit — the stop-motion stutter that makes everything jump in 12-frames-a-second steps instead of gliding, plus a constant hand-held wobble and a way for things to spring into place.
  • A film-treatment look — grain, scan lines, a vignette and a warm grade, layered over every scene so it reads like aged film instead of a clean digital render.

First — the Remotion project + motion engine

✦Claude CodePrompt · Remotion setup
▸Set up a new Remotion project for a vertical documentary reel — 1080×1920 at 30fps, with one composition and one file per scene so I can build and preview each on its own, plus a shared engine file that every scene imports. In that shared file, give me the motion helpers I'll reuse everywhere: a posterize-time helper that snaps the current frame to 12fps steps so movement stutters like stop-motion instead of gliding; a boil wobble; a slow drift; a pingpong oscillator; and a spring entrance for things popping into place. Set it up so I can drop each finished scene onto one main timeline later.

The posterize step is the single highest-leverage line in the whole engine. It's four lines of code, it costs nothing, and it's the difference between "a web animation" and "someone shot this". Watch the same move with it off and on:

12fps · posterized

Same easing, same duration. The right one just samples time in 12fps chunks.

Then — the film look

This is the part that turns a clean digital render into something that feels like aged film — and on a story about film, it's doing double duty as subject matter. Toggle the layers on:

✦Claude CodePrompt · Film look
▸Build the film-treatment look — a reusable wrapper I can wrap any scene in so it reads like aged film. This one's a story about film, so the treatment isn't decoration — it's the subject. The textures are in my assets folder (grain.jpg, grunge.jpg). Layer these top to bottom with exactly these settings: Scan lines: vertical lines — a 1.6px black line at 16% opacity repeating every 8px, blurred by 0.7px. Texture sandwich: grain.jpg on multiply, inverted with brightness 1.35 and contrast 1.02, at 55% opacity; then grunge.jpg on colour-burn at 16% opacity. Vignette: a radial gradient — ellipse 92% by 82% centred at 50% / 48%, clear until 55%, fading to black at 50% opacity by the edge. Grade: a colour filter of saturate 0.9, contrast 1.06, sepia 0.2, brightness 0.95 — warmer and more Kodachrome than neutral. Expose those four as props so I can tweak per scene. Gate-weave: jitter the whole frame with a stepped wiggle at 12fps, about 5px of travel, and scale everything up 1.012 so the weave never reveals an edge. Sprocket edge (this reel only): an optional overlay of 35mm film perforations running down both edges — rounded-square holes about 26px tall repeating every 46px, at 12% opacity, sliding downward one hole every ~40 frames so the frame feels like it's being pulled through a gate. Default it off; I'll switch it on for the scenes that need it. Everything rides the engine's 12fps posterize step. Put a toggle on each layer so I can switch parts off.
This is the multiplier. You write it once and all six scenes inherit the stop-motion feel and the film look automatically — so every scene prompt after this can focus purely on its own choreography, never the treatment.

01Scene 1 — the invention

"In 1975, a young Kodak engineer built the world's first digital camera."

The opener's job is to plant the underdog and stop the scroll in the same three seconds. The move is the portal zoom-through: a black-and-white photo hangs framed on a museum wall, and the camera flies straight through the frame into the picture, which blooms into colour as it lands.

The trick that makes it work isn't the zoom — it's the weld. For the first two seconds the photo and the gold frame have to move as one rigid object. If they drift apart even slightly, the illusion dies instantly and it reads as two layers scaling at different rates. So you lock the content scale to a fixed multiple of the wall scale (0.369×), and only then, at frame 62, let them detach.

Two more things carry the shot. A motion-blur burst ramps 0 → 9px → 0 across frames 54/61/72, peaking exactly where the camera is fastest — that's what hides the seam at the detach. And the engineer never sits still: a character boil breathes his scale ±0.5% and sways him ±0.5°, snapped to the 12fps step, so a flat cut-out reads as alive.

02Scene 2 — the dismissal

"He showed his bosses. They told him it was cute — but don't tell anyone about it."

Scene 2 is the villain scene, and it's built exactly like the Blockbuster boardroom — a character on a sunburst, two props swinging in from opposite edges in hand-drawn diamonds, a number that slams, and a punctuation flash. Only the skin changed: the red sunburst became Kodak yellow, and the two props became the future in one hand, the cash cow in the other — the prototype and a roll of 35mm film.

The number that lands by being small

In the Netflix reel the slot machine slams on 50 MILLION and the joke is how cheap it was. Here it slams on 0.01 MEGAPIXELS and the joke is the opposite — how laughably bad the prototype was, and that they were still right to be wrong. Same rig, inverted punchline. Give it a beat to sit before you cut.

0.01

Frames 72–82: it spins past gigapixels and kilopixels, then slams. The shake on landing is two 2-frame steps — never a smooth settle.

The punctuation — a negative, not a glitch

The Netflix scene ends on a hold-keyframe invert that reads as a glitch. Here the exact same effect reads as a photographic negative — which is free thematic weight, so lean into it. Fire it in two or three bursts of 2–3 frames on hold keyframes (instant on, instant off — never a fade), then push the exec to black-and-white and bloom darkroom-red safelight out of his eyes.

Both are hold keyframes. If either one eases, it stops reading as a camera artefact.

03Scene 3 — the opposite

"So everyone else built the opposite. No film. No prints. No waiting."

Three negations in the line, so three objects on screen — this scene writes itself. Front pages spring onto a desk from three different directions: left, right, bottom. Directions matter more than they sound. Three papers entering from the same edge reads as a slideshow; three papers entering from three edges reads as a montage.

The one number to get right is the spring. Stiffness ~42 with mass ~1.1 gives you a ~0.9s glide that settles with a small bounce. Crank the stiffness and they snap in like UI toasts; that's the giveaway that a computer made it.

The addition on this reel: as each paper lands, scatter a few spent 35mm negatives underneath at random angles, springing on the same frame at 0.6× the travel. The desk fills with the dead format while the headlines announce the new one — the whole thesis of the scene, in the background, without a word.

04Scene 4 — the fall

"By 2012, Kodak had filed for bankruptcy and switched off its film plants for good."

Two flat images. No 3D, no camera projection, no depth pass. The entire sense of dimension in this shot comes from one decision: the man scales faster than the building. Building goes 1.0 → 1.12; man goes 1.0 → 1.7. That mismatch is what your eye reads as "he's closer than the wall."

And the anchor is everything. Both scale around one ground point at his feet (x560 / y1690), not the frame centre. Scale from the centre and he slides up off the pavement like a sticker.

The shadow is the man

There is no shadow asset. The shadow is the character cut-out — duplicated, painted pure black, flipped down from the feet and skewed so it lies along the ground. Build it in three moves:

skewX(0°)  ·  the reel uses −53° at 55% opacity, 7px blur

The last beat is the cheapest and the best: on frame 96, a bank of lit windows on the plant snaps to black on a single hold keyframe. No fade. One frame, and the building is dead — it does more for the line than any amount of camera move.

05Scene 5 — the payoff

"That same year, Instagram sold for a billion dollars — thirteen employees, not one roll of film."

This is the money shot, and it's the one place the Kodak reel diverges emotionally from the Netflix one. There the payoff belongs to the hero. Here it belongs to somebody else entirely — thirteen people in a rented loft, cashing the cheque Kodak declined to write. So the room is deliberately cheap: brick, cable, one hanging bulb. The contrast with a hundred-year-old empire is the point.

The lamp is drawn, not photographed

There is no light in the plate. The glow is four stacked radial gradients on screen blend plus one blurred funnel beam, all parented to the lamp so they swing with it. Build them up one at a time and watch it turn into light:

layers: none — the plate as shot

Hit Defocus with the layers on: the hot core blooms to 2.4× into a bokeh disc. Skip that and the glow looks pasted-on the moment the lens softens.

The phone flicker uses hold keyframes only — on 23–28, off 29–31, on 32–61, off 62–63, on 64–71, gone. Instant on, instant off. Fade any of those and it stops reading as a screen and starts reading as a lamp.

06Scene 6 — the drawer

"Kodak invented the future, then locked it in a drawer."

Don't build this one. Clone Scene 4 and reskin it. The parallax punch, the cast shadow, the camera push, the treatment, the posterize — all of it is already right, and rebuilding it from scratch will only get you a finale that doesn't quite match the fall it's echoing. The whole prompt is a diff.

What changes: the character becomes the engineer, decades older, holding the same prototype. The background goes back to the Scene 1 lab, now stripped and empty — we end where we started. He stays centred; no shift this time. And one new element carries the punchline.

The drawer

The Netflix reel ends on a foam finger wagging "no" — a decaying oscillation about a wrist. This one ends on a filing drawer slamming shut, which is the same rig with translation swapped in for rotation: 34px × cos(t × 0.6) × e^(−0.05t). It overshoots, bounces back, settles. Then a dust puff out of the seam, and one 2-frame negative blink to end the reel.

The decay envelope is what sells it as metal. Constant-amplitude oscillation reads as a cartoon; exponential decay reads as mass.

Where they come from

Nothing here gets drawn from scratch. Three sources cover the whole sheet:

  • Reference photography — the things that really exist: a 1970s lab bench, a shuttered industrial plant, a brick loft, a wooden desk. Search them, don't generate them; real photographs carry detail that generators smooth away.
  • An image generator — everything that has to be invented: all four characters, the three fake front pages, the prototype and film-roll props. Generate, then background-remove into clean cut-outs.
  • Edit-pack collections — the finishing pieces you'd never bother generating: the film grain and grunge washes, the gold frame, the lamp, the smoke plume on black, the filing drawer.
Cut-outs, not scenes. Every character has to come back as a transparent PNG on its own — not standing in a generated room. The whole depth illusion in Scenes 4 and 6 depends on scaling the character independently of the background, and you can't do that if they're baked into one image.

The effects follow the same rule as the visuals: this is a story about cameras, so every hit should sound like a moving part. Mechanical, not synthetic.

✦ElevenLabsSound effects
▸Generate each effect from a short text description in Sound Effects mode — keep them tight and punchy: Whooshes: "fast cinematic whoosh transition" — a long one for the zoom-through, a quick one for pans. Camera shutter: "vintage 35mm camera shutter, single snap" — the museum-photo opener. Film advance: "manual film advance lever ratchet, mechanical" — under the slot-machine spin. Projector clatter: "16mm film projector running, steady mechanical clatter" — a low loop under Scene 3. Fluorescent cut: "fluorescent tube switching off, electrical thunk" — the one-frame window kill in Scene 4. Notification burst: "short digital notification chime, bright" — retrigger on each phone flicker. Drawer slam: "metal filing cabinet drawer slamming shut, heavy, with rattle" — the finale. Loudest thing in the reel; everything else ducks under it. Then trim each clip to its attack so the hit is on the beat, never a frame early.
Mix so the words always win. Music sits under the VO, not beside it. Duck the bed 6–9dB under every spoken line, and let it come back up only in the gaps. If you can hear the track over the narration on phone speakers, it's too loud.

Takeaways

  • The voiceover is the source code. Six lines decided every shot, every caption, every cut point in this reel. Lock the script first, always.
  • Build the engine once, reskin forever. This whole reel is the Netflix reel's engine with a new story poured into it. The second one costs a fraction of the first — and the third costs less than that.
  • Depth is animation, not layers. Two flat plates and one shared ground anchor, with the character scaling faster than the wall. That's the entire trick in Scenes 4 and 6.
  • Hold keyframes for anything physical. The negative flash, the phone flicker, the window kill — instant on, instant off. The moment one of them eases, it stops reading as a camera and starts reading as CSS.
  • Let the theme do free work. On a story about film, the grain, the sprocket holes and the negative flash aren't decoration — they're the subject. Pick a topic where your treatment already means something.
  • Retell, don't invent. Everyone knows the Kodak story. Telling it better than the last person is the entire job.
